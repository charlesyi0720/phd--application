\section{Motivation}

Three facts have emerged from the first wave of evidence on generative AI and the labour market.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \textbf{Young workers are falling behind.} Using ADP payroll records, \citet{brynjolfsson2026canaries} find that employment of 22--25 year olds in the most AI-exposed occupations is about 19\% below the path of their less-exposed peers by mid-2026, up from 15\% a year earlier. In administrative Census data, \citet{tucker2026} finds that early-career employment in the most exposed quintile of industry--state cells fell by 12\% over the ten quarters after ChatGPT's release.
  \item \textbf{The adjustment runs through hiring.} In both data sets, and in résumé data on firms that adopt generative AI \citep{hosseini2026}, the decline is driven by fewer hires of juniors rather than more separations.
  \item \textbf{Experienced workers show no comparable decline.} Senior employment in the same occupations and firms has held up, and grows faster in occupations that rely on tacit knowledge \citep{brynjolfsson2026canaries, hosseini2026}.
\end{enumerate}

These facts are descriptive, and their interpretation is contested. Economy-wide effects on earnings and hours appear small \citep{humlum2026}, and part of the decline in youth hiring coincides with the 2022--23 monetary tightening \citep{tucker2026}. But if the facts reflect a genuine shift in the demand for junior labour, they raise a dynamic question that the existing evidence cannot answer. Junior positions are where workers acquire the tacit knowledge that later makes them senior \citep{garicano2006}. If AI removes the bottom rungs of the career ladder, today's lost entry-level jobs are only the first cost. The rest arrives later, through slower skill accumulation for the affected cohorts and, eventually, a thinner supply of the experienced workers whom AI complements. \citet{ide2026} shows in theory that automating entry-level tasks can lower long-run growth through exactly this channel. How large that channel is in practice remains unknown.

% =====================================================================
\section{Research Questions}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Q\arabic*.}]
  \item How much of the post-2022 decline in junior employment in AI-exposed occupations is caused by AI, as opposed to monetary policy, remote work or rising educational attainment?
  \item How large are the losses in experience and lifetime earnings for cohorts entering the labour market after 2022, and when, if ever, does reduced junior hiring translate into a shortage of senior workers?
  \item Which of entry-level wage subsidies, subsidised apprenticeships and support for switching occupations restores skill accumulation at the lowest fiscal cost?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Literature and Contribution}

\textit{Empirical work on AI and labour markets} documents exposure \citep{eloundou2024, gmyrek2023} and early employment effects \citep{brynjolfsson2026canaries, tucker2026, hosseini2026, humlum2026}. These studies are reduced-form and estimate relative effects; they cannot recover cohort-level welfare or the general-equilibrium path of senior labour supply. \textit{Dynamic models of occupational choice} quantify how workers reallocate after demand shocks, mostly trade shocks \citep{keane1997, artuc2010, dixcarneiro2014, traiberman2019}, but, to my knowledge, have not been applied to AI, nor built around the junior--senior distinction. \textit{Theories of knowledge hierarchies and on-the-job learning} \citep{garicano2000, garicano2006, ide2026, friebel2026} explain why entry-level work matters for skill formation, but are not taken to data.

I embed the junior--senior skill-accumulation channel in an estimable dynamic model of occupational choice and discipline it with the new micro evidence on early-career hiring. The model then answers what none of these strands can answer alone: how much senior skill the post-2022 entry cohorts will fail to accumulate, and what it would cost to restore it.

\section{Model}

\paragraph{Workers.} Workers live from age 22 and choose each year among occupations $o \in \{1,\dots,O\}$ or non-employment. A worker's state is $s=(o, e, a)$: current occupation, accumulated experience in that occupation, and age. Experience grows only while employed, and a worker becomes senior in occupation $o$ once experience passes a threshold $\bar{e}_o$; with occupation-specific experience, switching occupations destroys part of the accumulated skill \citep{keane1997, dixcarneiro2014}. Entering a junior position in occupation $k$ succeeds with probability $\lambda_{k,t}$. The value of state $s$ is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V_t(s) = w_t(s) + \beta\, \mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{k}\big\{ & \lambda_{k,t+1}V_{t+1}(k,e',a+1) \\
 & + (1-\lambda_{k,t+1})\,U_{t+1}(e',a+1) - C_{ok} + \varepsilon_{k}\big\}\Big],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $w_t(s)$ is the wage, $U$ the value of non-employment, $C_{ok}$ the cost of switching from $o$ to $k$, and $\varepsilon_k$ an idiosyncratic preference shock. For incumbents, $\lambda=1$ for staying put.

\paragraph{Firms and AI.} Output in occupation $o$ combines junior labour $L^{J}$, senior labour $L^{S}$ and AI services $M$:
\begin{equation}
Y_{o,t} = \Big[\alpha_o \big(X^{J}_{o,t}\big)^{\rho} + (1-\alpha_o)\big(L^{S}_{o,t}\big)^{\rho}\Big]^{1/\rho}, \qquad X^{J}_{o,t} = \Big[\big(L^{J}_{o,t}\big)^{\sigma} + \big(\theta_{o,t}M_{o,t}\big)^{\sigma}\Big]^{1/\sigma}, \qquad \rho < \sigma < 1.
\end{equation}
AI performs the codified tasks of juniors and enters the junior input $X^{J}$; its efficiency $\theta_{o,t}=\theta_t E_o$ rises after 2022 in proportion to the occupation's exposure $E_o$. Because $\sigma>\rho$, AI substitutes for juniors more readily than the junior input substitutes for seniors, so a rise in $\theta_{o,t}$ lowers the demand for junior labour and raises the marginal product of seniors.

\paragraph{Hiring frictions.} If junior wages fully cleared the market, the model would predict falling entry wages rather than falling hiring, which contradicts fact (ii). I therefore assume entry-level wages adjust sluggishly. Firms hire juniors until their marginal product equals the sticky entry wage, and the job-finding rate $\lambda_{o,t}$ equals these hires divided by the number of applicants, so a decline in junior labour demand appears mainly as a fall in $\lambda_{o,t}$. Because experience is portable across firms within an occupation, the hiring firm does not capture the value of the skills a junior acquires, and with rigid entry wages the entrant cannot pay for them by accepting lower pay. Junior hiring is then too low from a social point of view, which creates a role for policy \citep{ide2026}.

\paragraph{Key mechanism.} An AI shock lowers $\lambda_{o,t}$ for young workers in exposed occupations. Some delay entry, some enter less-exposed occupations where their experience is worth less, and some accumulate experience more slowly. Years later, the stock of seniors in exposed occupations is smaller than it would otherwise have been, so senior wages rise.

\section{Identification and Estimation}

Estimation proceeds in two steps. First, I estimate the parameters that predate generative AI from data before 2020. Switching costs come from gross occupational flows in the CPS, using the conditional-choice-probability approach of \citet{artuc2010}, which recovers them from flows and wages without repeatedly solving the full model. Returns to experience and promotion thresholds require following the same workers for many years, which the CPS cannot do, so I estimate them from age--earnings profiles in long panels: the PSID for the US and HILDA for Australia. The complementarity parameter $\rho$ and the weights $\alpha_o$ come from pre-2020 variation in the relative wages and relative employment of junior and senior workers within occupations.

Second, I identify the path of AI efficiency $\theta_t$ from the post-2022 divergence in hiring between junior and senior workers within the same occupation, across occupations with different exposure. Monetary tightening also falls hardest on entry hiring, so the junior--senior comparison alone does not remove it. The identifying assumption is that, absent AI, the junior--senior hiring gap would not have widened more in more exposed occupations once each occupation's exposure to high-frequency monetary policy shocks \citep{jarocinski2020} is controlled for, following \citet{hosseini2026}. Two moments are left out of estimation as out-of-sample tests of the mechanism: the post-2022 change in the senior wage premium and in promotion rates.

\section{Data}

The US data are the Current Population Survey (CPS), the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) and the Quarterly Workforce Indicators (QWI); the Australian data are the HILDA Survey and the ABS Labour Force Survey (LFS). All are public or accessible to university researchers. Australia provides a useful second case: a smaller economy that adopts the same technology with a different wage-setting system, where award wages may make entry-level wages especially rigid.

\section{Preliminary Work}

In my Honours thesis, supervised by Associate Professor Jan Kab\'{a}tek, I link restricted-release HILDA data for 2015--2024 to the occupation-level exposure scores of \citet{jsa2025}, which adapt the method of \citet{gmyrek2023} to four-digit Australian occupations and separate automation from augmentation exposure. Using two-way fixed effects and event-study designs, I find that after ChatGPT's release, job insecurity rose more in occupations with higher augmentation exposure: a one-standard-deviation difference is associated with a 0.63 percentage point larger rise, 7.4\% of the 2022 mean. No comparable gradient appears for automation exposure.

The thesis also identified the limitation that motivates this proposal. It measures how \emph{incumbent} workers perceive their risk, whereas the largest effects so far fall on \emph{entrants}, who are never observed in a sample of the already employed. And in US data the sharpest decline in young workers' employment occurs where AI substitutes for human tasks \citep{brynjolfsson2026canaries}, the automation-type exposure that showed no gradient among Australian incumbents. This is consistent with adjustment through hiring rather than separations: incumbents keep their jobs while fewer entrants are hired. This project therefore moves from incumbents' perceptions to entrants' hiring and skill accumulation. It builds directly on the thesis's Australian exposure measures and HILDA infrastructure, and the finding that workers' expectations responded to AI within two years matters for a forward-looking model, in which today's occupational choices depend on beliefs about future demand.

\section{Policy Counterfactuals}

The estimated model will be used to compute, for each cohort entering between 2023 and 2030, the loss in experience and lifetime earnings relative to a no-AI path; the timing and size of any senior shortage; and the cost-effectiveness of entry-level wage subsidies, apprenticeship subsidies and occupational-switching support.

The main risk is that the decline in junior hiring proves largely cyclical. Even a temporary hiring freeze leaves the cohorts caught in it with less experience \citep{friebel2026}, which is what the model measures, and the reduced-form evidence separating AI from monetary policy would itself be a contribution.
